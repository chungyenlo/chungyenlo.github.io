## Job Market Paper

**Concentration without Greater Market Power? Evidence from U.S. Sugar’s Acquisition of Imperial Sugar (Draft upon request)**

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract</b></summary>


The Department of Justice sued to block U.S. Sugar’s acquisition of Imperial Sugar, arguing that it would raise the price of refined sugar in the Southeast. This paper asks whether retail sugar prices in that region rose as the merger of the two refiners moved from announcement through litigation to closing, and why they did not. Using NielsenIQ Retail Scanner data from 2016 to 2023, I compare the Southeast with the rest of the country in each phase of the case, including the first full year after closing, and find no relative price increase in any of them. A small relative decline during the litigation, concentrated in store brands, largely disappears in the year after closing, and it never appears within a retail chain. I then estimate a mixed logit demand model and recover markups under multiproduct Bertrand competition. Measured over the firms that set retail prices the market is highly concentrated, with a Herfindahl–Hirschman index averaging 2,792, but the transaction changes that index by 0.2 points, against the 100-point threshold in the 2023 Merger Guidelines, and diversion from the acquired brands to the acquirer’s is 2.6 percent outside the Southeast and 0.4 percent within it. These screens measure only the change in who sets retail prices. Because a merger of refiners could also reach the shelf through the cost of sugar, I separate retail prices into recovered costs and markups. Costs in the Southeast do not rise relative to the rest of the country in any phase, and markups are no higher after the announcement than before. The evidence complements rather than tests the government’s case about wholesale customers.
</details>
