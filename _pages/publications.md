## Job Market Paper

**Concentration without Greater Market Power? Evidence from U.S. Sugar’s Acquisition of Imperial Sugar (Draft upon request)**

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract</b></summary>
The Department of Justice sued to block U.S. Sugar's acquisition of Imperial Sugar on the ground that concentration in refining was already high. This paper asks what the transaction looks like from the retail market where the two firms' brands meet, and finds the two standard merger screens in sharp disagreement. I study the acquisition, announced in March 2021 and completed in November 2022, using NielsenIQ Retail Scanner data from 2016 to 2022. Measured over the firms that set retail prices the market is highly concentrated, with a Herfindahl-Hirschman index averaging 2,849 nationally, but the transaction changes that index by 0.2 points, against the 100-point threshold at which the 2023 Merger Guidelines presume harm. A difference-in-differences design finds no relative price increase in the Southeast, where the merging firms' refining capacity is concentrated. I then estimate a mixed logit demand model and recover markups under multiproduct Bertrand competition. Diversion from the acquired brands to the acquirer's is 2.6 percent outside the Southeast and 0.5 percent within it, close to what proportional substitution would deliver, and the implied upward pricing pressure is 2.7 and 0.4 percent; markups and Lerner indices are no higher after the announcement than before. The merging firms are small downstream while the market they sell into is concentrated, so a screen built on the level of concentration flags this transaction and one built on diversion does not, and the retail data say the second was right. What matters is the merging firms' position in the market consumers buy from, not the concentration of the stage where the deal occurs.
</details>
