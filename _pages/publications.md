## Job Market Paper

**Concentration without Greater Market Power? Evidence from U.S. Sugar’s Acquisition of Imperial Sugar (Draft upon request)**

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract</b></summary>
This paper examines whether a merger between sugar refiners affects competition in the retail markets where their brands compete. I study U.S. Sugar’s acquisition of Imperial Sugar, announced in March 2021, using NielsenIQ Retail Scanner data from 2016 to 2022. A difference-in-differences design comparing the Southeast, where the merging firms’ refining capacity is concentrated, with other regions finds no relative increase in retail prices following the announcement. I then estimate a mixed logit demand model and recover markups and marginal costs under multiproduct Bertrand competition. Substitution is spread across many competing brands rather than concentrated among the merging parties’ products. Taking the ownership changes as known, a counterfactual decomposition separates the ownership and cost channels within a common demand environment. The change in ownership raises equilibrium prices by 0.03 percent and leaves markups, profits, consumer surplus, and welfare essentially unchanged, while differences between the pre- and post-announcement cost environments lower prices by 0.4 percent and markups by 2.1 percent. The merging firms together account for under 4 percent of retail volume, and private label, priced by retailers, accounts for most of the category, so the transaction alters the ownership of only a small share of the products consumers choose among. The results suggest that the competitive consequences of a merger among producers depend less on the change in producer concentration than on how much of the consumer-facing market the merging firms control and on how readily buyers substitute between the products they own.
</details>
